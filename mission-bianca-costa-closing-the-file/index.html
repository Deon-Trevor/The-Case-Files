Mission: Bianca Costa - Closing the File

By Sync_Pundit Updated: Jul 24, 2026 Closed
CASE 2026-11 - opened

Two years ago I opened a file on a Twitter account using Bianca Costa's photo as its brand - a "catfish," in its own words, worn as a badge.

How It Started

The theft predates my involvement by years, and the early history comes from Bianca herself, on the record in a sit-down interview with Penuel The Black Pen (now The Penuel Show). Her sister found the account first, back when it had around 30,000 followers, and asked it to remove Bianca's photo - then rallied friends to report it too. The account's answer to that pressure was not compliance. It escalated: Bianca's photo went up on the banner as well, alongside the avatar. Only then did Bianca contact the account directly and ask it to stop. It didn't.

The original mission had three explicit objectives - vindication for Bianca, a bias test on Twitter's enforcement, and a refined takedown playbook. The debrief on all three is at the end of this file.

The Bio, Across Four Snapshots

  • 2020–2021 - the early reports

  • The account (created January 2020) grows on Bianca's photo. Her sister's takedown requests are answered with escalation - her image added to the banner too.
  • Oct 14, 2021 - baseline
  • Archived bio: "The only legal Catfish 🔥🇿🇦 .. BLACK TWITTER President." Her photo as the avatar. No disguise, no euphemism.
  • Jul 20, 2022 - after her takedown requests
  • Same bio, word for word - still spelled out in plain English - now sitting on 1.1M followers. Defiance, not denial.
  • Jun 9, 2023 - when I opened this case
  • The word "Catfish" is gone, replaced with 🐈🐟. Same sentence, same claim, laundered through emoji to slip past keyword moderation. A "commentary" tag quietly appended, a paid checkmark acquired, 1.7M followers. Still her photo.
  • Jun 30, 2026 - current state
  • The avatar is no longer Bianca. It's an AI-generated face - close enough to preserve brand recognition, different enough to no longer be her. The bio now reads "fan account." 3.5M followers. The brand survived. The theft didn't.

Evidence Log

2021-10-14  "The only legal Catfish 🔥🇿🇦"
2022-07-20  "The only legal Catfish 🔥🇿🇦"              ← unchanged after takedown requests
2023-06-09  "The only legal 🐈🐟🔥🇿🇦 .. \"commentary\""  ← keyword swapped for emoji
2026-06-30  "...fan account \"we stand with brothers in difficult time\""

Every edit in that log is a tell. You don't reword a joke four times over five years unless someone keeps making you. Moderation systems flag words, not emoji, and the edits track platform pressure precisely - the claim never changed, only its ability to be searched for.

This Was Never Just Annoying

The interview put real costs on the record, and they deserve to be in this file. Brand campaign managers read comment sections; when a sponsored post's comments fill up with references to an impersonator account instead of the product, brands walk. Bianca described deals lost over exactly this - including one pulled the same week the interview was recorded. Strangers - once, a child - approached her in public and addressed her by the account's name instead of her own. Years of it.

"It's like I don't exist anymore." - Bianca Costa, interview with Penuel The Black Pen

And she said something that impersonation victims rarely say out loud: it put her in therapy. She spent sessions working through what it does to your sense of self when three and a half million people associate your face with words you never wrote.

What Changed the Outcome

Not an apology. Not a bio rewrite the account chose on its own. Platform enforcement - the lever her sister started pulling when the account had 30K followers, that Bianca pulled herself, that I pulled with the evidence pack in the original case file - finally landed on the only asset that mattered: the photo. Everything else about the account's brand, audience, and tone survived intact. Only the piece that was never its to use is gone.

That's not total justice. I am NOT satisfied. It took five years, a family's worth of abuse reports, a public interview, and sustained platform pressure to remove one stolen photograph from one account - while that account grew from 30K followers to 3.5M and picked up a paid checkmark along the way. But the file closes on a fact that wasn't true in 2021: whoever looks at that account today is not looking at Bianca Costa.

Debrief - The Original Objectives

The original file declared three objectives for this mission. Closing a case means scoring them honestly.

  • Objective 1 - Vindication for Bianca ✓ Achieved, eventually. Twitter/X finally actioned it: her photo is off the avatar and banner. Anyone who finds the real Bianca today is finding her, not the account that wore her face for five years.
  • Objective 2 - Test for enforcement bias ✓ (bias confirmed) The result is unambiguous. Across the takedowns I run, the same class of report - same evidence standard, same ToS violation - gets actioned quickly when the offender is a nobody. When the offender has millions of followers and a paid checkmark, it takes years of sustained pressure. High-value accounts don't get equal enforcement; they get a negotiation. The bio's five-year shape-shift documented above only works if the platform is grading engagement, not compliance.
  • Objective 3 - Refine the takedown process ✓ (with an asterisk) My process came out sharper: evidence packs built from archived snapshots beat screenshots, likeness claims beat keyword reports, and persistence across ownership changes matters more than any single filing. But the counterparty got worse. The quality and speed of X's responses dropped hard between the Twitter era and now - slower replies, more form-letter dead ends, less human review. The refined process works; it just works against a degraded abuse desk.

In that interview, Bianca and Penuel closed on a demand I'll co-sign as the last line of this case: platforms should require a verified human identity behind any account making personal claims in someone else's face - and treat refusal as grounds for removal. Until that's the rule, every takedown like this one will take five years too long.
